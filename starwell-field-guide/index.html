Starwell SI · Field guide for HVAC visits

Diagnose before you prescribe.

A good HVAC tech never quotes a new compressor over the phone. He checks the pressures first. This guide teaches you to do the same with a business: ask, listen, find the real problem, check if AI can fix it, and promise only what you can deliver.

7 stops plus a field cardAbout 20 minutes to readReview the field card before every visit

Stop 1 of 7 · Mindset

Be the tech, not the salesperson.

On the first visit, you have one job: understand the business so well that the owner says, "That's right." You're not there to sell anything yet.

Think of it like a service call

The tech listens to the homeowner, checks the system, finds the cause, and then quotes. The salesperson walks in with a unit already picked out. Owners have met plenty of salespeople. Be the tech.

Why this works on people

Respect opens people up

Criticize how someone runs their shop and they defend it. Ask about it with real interest and they explain it, including the messy parts.

Dale Carnegie, 1936: "Become genuinely interested in other people."

Talking feels good

Brain scans show that sharing about ourselves activates the brain's reward system. When the owner talks, they enjoy the visit, and you learn.

Tamir & Mitchell, Harvard, 2012

Follow-ups build trust

People who ask more follow-up questions are liked more. A follow-up proves you heard the last answer.

Huang et al., Harvard, 2017

Owners describe fixes, not problems

When an owner says "I need a new CRM" or "Can you build me a chatbot?", that's their guess at a fix. Your job is to find the problem underneath it.

They say

We need a chatbot on the website.

You ask

What would that fix for you?

Their answer might be "We miss calls at night." Now you're working on the real problem, and a chatbot might not even be the best fix.

Stop 2 of 7 · Ask

Climb the ladder: Day, Leak, Cost, Want.

Good questions move in order: what happens, what goes wrong, what it costs, and what they want instead. Skip a rung and you end up guessing.

After any answer, use a follow-up move

"Tell me more about that.""What happened next?""How often does that happen?""Can you give me a recent example?"Repeat their last three wordsThen stay quiet for three seconds

Silence feels awkward to you. To the owner, it feels like room to keep talking. The most useful thing they say often comes right after a pause.

Stop 3 of 7 · Listen

Listen for signals, then play it back.

The owner will tell you where the pain is, but rarely in the first answer. It shows up in what they repeat, where they sigh, the numbers they mention, and the workarounds they describe.

Six signals to listen for

RepeatsThe same topic three times is their real priority, even if they said something else first.
Workarounds"We just…" or "I usually stay late and…" points to a hidden manual process. These are prime spots for automation.
Energy shiftsA sigh, a laugh, a louder voice. Emotion marks the pain that matters to them personally.
NumbersCalls, hours, dollars, techs. Write every one down. You'll need them to figure out the cost.
One person holds it"Only Linda knows how" or "Everything goes through me" means a bottleneck and a risk.
"Always done it this way"Either a habit nobody questioned or something they're proud of. Ask gently to find out which.

Spot the signals

What a good listener heard

  1. Must-fix: after-hours calls are lost in peak season. He said "the phones" three times.
  2. Real pain: about 15 replacement quotes a week with no follow-up.
  3. Bottleneck: only Mike can price, so he does estimates at 10pm.

Notice he opened with "the busy season," but the real issue is unanswered calls. The signals pointed there.

Play it back so they know you heard them

"So what I'm hearing is the problem, in their words. It happens how often, and it costs you time, money or stress. Did I get that right?"

Example: "So what I'm hearing is that after five, calls go to voicemail, and in July that's every night. By morning half of those people have booked someone else. Did I get that right?"

You're aiming for "That's right." Negotiator Chris Voss points out that "That's right" means the person feels understood. "You're right" often just means they want you to stop talking.

Label the feeling

It sounds like evenings are where this really hurts.

Start with "It sounds like" or "It seems like." If you're wrong, they'll correct you and tell you more.

Mirror the last words

Owner: "…and it all goes to voicemail."
You: "Goes to voicemail?" Then wait.

Repeating their last few words as a question gets them to explain without you steering.

Stop 4 of 7 · Sort

A pebble or a cracked heat exchanger?

A squeaky blower is annoying. A cracked heat exchanger can leak carbon monoxide. Both are "problems," but only one is a must-fix. Sort before you solve.

Pebble

Annoying, but it doesn't cost real money or customers. Examples: the website font looks dated, or the shop radio is too loud.

VS

Must-fix

It loses money, customers or safety every week. Examples: calls going to voicemail in July, or quotes that never get followed up.

Trap 1: Loudest isn't largest

The thing they complain about most emotionally can still be a pebble. Check it with numbers before you treat it as the priority.

Trap 2: The silent must-fix

The biggest leaks are often things owners have accepted as "part of the job." They don't bring them up because they don't know they can be fixed. Ask:

What do you just put up with?

If you had one extra office person for free, what would you hand them first?

Find the bottleneck

A duct system moves only as much air as its tightest point allows. Speeding up any other part changes nothing. Business works the same way (Eliyahu Goldratt, The Goal, 1984). Tap each stage of an HVAC job to see how to spot a jam there and whether AI usually helps.

Symptom or cause? A frozen coil is a symptom. Low airflow or low refrigerant is the cause. "We miss calls" is a symptom. "Nobody is assigned to the phones after 5pm" is the cause. Keep asking "What causes that?" until you reach a process, not a person. Toyota made this habit famous as "ask why five times."

Stop 5 of 7 · Can AI fix it?

Check the panel before you install.

An electrician checks the panel before adding a circuit. Before you suggest any AI, check three things: is this the kind of work AI does well, can a mistake be caught, and can AI reach the information it needs.

Think of AI as a new office hire

Fast, tireless and available at 2am. Great at answering, reading, writing, sorting, summarizing and following clear steps. It needs training, rules and someone checking its work. It can't climb a ladder, can't take legal responsibility, and sometimes sounds confident when it's wrong.

Three checks before you suggest anything

Is it work done with words or data on a screen or a phone?

Answering, scheduling, writing, sorting, reminding: yes. Anything physical (installing, diagnosing at the unit): no.

Does it repeat with clear rules, and can a wrong answer be caught before it hurts?

A wrong reminder text is cheap. A wrong safety call or a wrong price on a $14,000 install is not.

Can we reach the information?

Where does the data live, and can we read it and write back to it? The plug-in ladder below answers this.

What usually fits in an HVAC shop

Good fit

  • Answer missed and after-hours calls, text back, take booking requests
  • Follow up on sent quotes
  • Review requests after a job
  • Maintenance plan and tune-up reminders
  • Turn tech notes or voice memos into clean invoices
  • Call summaries for the office
  • Answer common questions (hours, service area, financing)

With a person checking

  • Dispatch suggestions and customer ETA texts
  • Draft quotes from past jobs, owner approves
  • Payment reminders, with a person for disputes
  • Rebate paperwork drafts (for example Energize CT), with a person submitting
  • Screening job applicants

Keep it human

  • Gas smell or carbon monoxide calls: route to a person or 911 right away
  • Diagnosing equipment remotely
  • Final pricing decisions
  • Work that needs a license
  • Upset customers in a dispute

Ask the owner

  • "What software runs the business, and which plan are you on?"
  • "Who's the admin on that account?"
  • "Can you pull a report of last month's calls or jobs?"
  • "Is anything still on paper or a whiteboard?"

What an MCP is, in plain words

An open standard Anthropic introduced in 2024 that gives AI tools one common way to plug into software, like USB-C for AI. When an official one exists, setup is fast. When it doesn't, we use the API rung instead. Community-built versions work, but the software company doesn't support them, so test them carefully.

Blockers to check before you promise

Access

Their plan may not include the API, or the admin may not approve it.

Who can turn on integrations in your account?

Data

If the info is messy, on paper or in one person's head, AI has nothing to work with.

Where would I find last month's quotes?

Rules

In Connecticut, recording a call without everyone's consent can lead to a civil lawsuit. A recorded notice counts as consent, so any AI phone agent must say the call is recorded. This isn't legal advice, so have the owner confirm with their attorney. Source

Trust

Owners worry most about data security. Explain exactly what the tool can see and change.

What would you need to see to feel safe with this?

Cost

API add-ons, phone minutes and software fees add up. Compare them to the cost you calculated.

What would this need to save to be worth it?

People

If the office manager feels replaced, the tool won't get used. Bring them in early.

Who on your team would use this every day?

Stop 6 of 7 · Promise

Quote after the diagnosis.

Trust takes months to build and one broken promise to lose. Promise less than you can deliver, then deliver more. A good tech says "about two hours" and finishes in ninety minutes.

Sort every idea into three buckets

Say this to the owner out loud. It shows you're honest about what AI can do, and it's the fastest way to earn trust.

We know we can

This part works today. We've seen it work.

Example: answering after-hours calls and texting you a summary.

We think we can

This should work. Here's how we'll test it first.

Example: booking straight into your schedule, once we confirm your plan allows it.

We wouldn't

I'd keep this human, and here's why.

Example: gas smell calls, final pricing, or customers in a dispute.

Prove it small first

Measure todayGet the starting number. For example, missed calls over the last two weeks.
Pick one problemThe must-fix with the clearest number. Just one.
Run it two weeksA person checks the AI's work every day at first.
CompareSame number, before and after. No guessing.
Decide togetherKeep it, adjust it or stop. The owner decides.

What to say instead

Never saySay this
"AI will handle everything.""AI handles the first reply. Your team handles the rest."
"It's 100% accurate.""It will make mistakes. Here's how we'll catch them."
"Set it and forget it.""We'll check it every week for the first month."
"You won't need your office manager.""Your office manager gets her Friday afternoons back."
"We can connect to anything.""Let me check what your software allows before I promise."
"It'll be live tomorrow.""Here's a realistic timeline, and what could slow it down."
