Why we're starting with HVAC

These shops keep Connecticut warm in January and cool in July. Their busiest moments come when the weather turns, which is exactly when the phone, the website and being easy to find matter most.

  1. They're experts at the work. Most owners came up through the trade. The website, the search results and the after-hours phone are rarely where they want to spend their night. That's where we come in.
  2. We don't sell AI. We take jobs off the owner's plate: being found, looking as good as their work, and never missing a call.
  3. We start local. Fairfield and New Haven County first, family-run shops of roughly 10 to 45 people, often with the owner still picking up the phone.

Psychology · you, the caller

Your confidence kit

, here's what the research says about the person on your end of the phone. Most of it is good news.

1. They'll like you more than you think
Across five studies, people after a first conversation consistently underestimated how much the other person liked them. It's called the liking gap (Boothby et al., 2018). The call felt shakier to you than it sounded to them. Transferred
2. Call the butterflies "excited"
People who said "I'm excited" before singing, speaking or doing math felt more excited, and in the original studies performed better than people who said "I'm calm" (Brooks, 2014). Honest note: a 2025 replication confirmed the feeling, but observers didn't rate speeches better. It reliably helps how you feel; the performance boost is less certain. Transferred
3. Your voice is the first demo
People judge warmth and competence from very short slices of behavior (Ambady et al., thin-slice studies of sales managers). On the phone your voice is the whole picture. Slow down, smile while you talk, pause after you say why you're calling.
4. You're holding a gift
You aren't asking for a favor. You're offering a finished package the owner keeps either way. That's a giving posture, and it sounds like one.
5. "Not now" is about timing
Most owners are mid-job when you call. A no usually means "not this minute." Thank them, end kindly, and move on with your energy intact.
6. Rhythm beats mood
Jeb Blount: prospect every day. Block the time, dial through it, log each call within the hour. Momentum does the rest.

Psychology · connection

People like people who like them

When you've done your homework, the owner hears it in your first sentence: I see your shop, I respect your time, I'm here for you.

What the research says

  • Transferred Reciprocity of liking: people tend to like those they believe like them (Montoya & Insko, 2008).
  • Transferred Shared ground: a meta-analysis of 313 studies found perceived similarity drives liking (Montoya, Horton & Kirchner, 2008). Local, trades, family business: use it when it's real.
  • Direct In B2B: buyers' trust in a salesperson was tied to expertise, likability and similarity (Doney & Cannon, 1997). Honest note: that trust didn't decide the immediate purchase once past performance was counted. Liking opens the door; the quality of the package walks through it.
  • Transferred Say where you found it: personal details landed well when people knew how they were gathered, and felt invasive when hidden (Aguirre et al., 2015). So: "I was on your website this morning…"
Know the shop, not the person's private life. Website, Google profile and reviews, business social pages, careers page, local news. Never home, family, or personal accounts.

Three moves after he answers

  1. One follow-up. "You said Sarah handles callbacks. What happens on her day off?" Follow-up questions increase liking (Huang et al., 2017).
  2. Ask for his expertise. "Thirty years fixing furnaces: what do homeowners get wrong when they call?" People who ask for advice are seen as more competent (Brooks et al., 2015).
  3. Let him do the math. "What's a typical no-heat repair worth to you?" Never supply the number yourself.

Our way with every question

Answer like a good steward

Every question an owner asks is a gift: it means he's thinking about it. We answer the same way every time, with honesty, warmth and care for his shop.

The four-step answer

  1. Thank. "Great question." He should feel smart for asking.
  2. Truth. The plain answer, including what we don't know yet.
  3. Tie. Connect it back to his shop and what he told you.
  4. Turn. Hand it back with a gentle question or the next small step.
Begin in a friendly way. Warmth first, always.
Let him do most of the talking. Be a good listener.
Avoid arguments. You can't win one, even when you're right.
Respect his opinion. Never say "you're wrong."
If we're wrong, admit it quickly.
Talk in terms of his interests, and see it from his side.

When he asks "what's it cost?"

Price questions are good news

If he's asking about price on day one, he's picturing using it. Answer it straight. Never dodge it.

What to say

Great question, and I'll be straight with you. The package we built is free, and it stays yours. If you ever want us to keep it running or take more off your plate, that's priced per shop, because every shop is set up differently, and we're still early. So we'd rather see it work for you first. If it does, you'll get a clear written quote before anything, with no surprises. And if it doesn't make sense for you, you keep everything.
Thank, truth, tie, turn. Free stays free, the "it depends" is explained, and he's in control.

If he pushes for a number

Fair enough. I'd rather give you a real number than guess and be wrong. After fifteen minutes on your setup, you'll have it in writing within a day.
Team to set: once StarWell sets a starting range for ongoing help, say it here plainly ("most shops start around…"). Research says an honest, fairly narrow range builds trust; a very wide one doesn't.

What the price depends on (say it plainly)

  • How many locations and trucks he runs.
  • How many calls come in, and when.
  • How much he wants us to run for him.

Never

  • Dodge it, or say "we'll get to that."
  • Make up a number to sound sure.
  • Discount on the spot, or say "only if you sign today."
  • Let "free" quietly turn into a bill.

What StarWell stands for

How we keep our word

A person makes every cold call. In February 2024 the FCC ruled that AI-generated voices count as artificial voices under the TCPA, so we never cold call with our voice agent. The demo line only answers when the owner calls it.

Your first week

Agree who calls and when. Pick 20 shops from the CT list. Confirm package turnaround with our builder. Set up the tracker.
Build 20 Prospect Briefs. Role-play all three scripts, the price question and the owner question library.
First calling block, early morning before the trucks leave (our guess, untested). Say "I'm excited." Log every call within the hour.
Second block, late afternoon. Send every recap email within the hour of each call.
Hold walkthroughs. Then review together: what got owners talking, what didn't, hours per package. Celebrate the learning goals you hit. Pick test 1 for week two.

The call on one card

  1. Opener: name, StarWell, "this is a cold call," where you looked.
  2. One true thing: a checkable fact about their shop.
  3. Their week: a question about something real. Listen. One follow-up.
  4. The gift: lead with what fits, in owner words. Why free. One limit.
  5. Email + date: recap within the hour, 15-minute walkthrough.

Why free: "We're new to Connecticut HVAC, and we'd rather show you than tell you. If you love it, maybe we work together down the road. If not, it's still yours."

Remember: they like you more than you think. Every dial is a rep.

Always: true facts, owner words, no pressure, a no is respected.
