The honest headline

Almost no peer-reviewed research studies cold calling directly. Only two of our twenty approaches rest on direct sales evidence, and that evidence comes from field sales, not cold calls. About eight more rest on solid research from other settings. The rest are practitioner data or our own hypotheses.

Read the labels

Direct B2B sales research
The strongest we have. Example: a meta-analysis of 155 samples and 31,000+ salespeople on adaptive selling. Lean on it.
Transferred Research from another setting
Solid studies (advertising, health goals, conversations) applied to calls. It probably carries over, but nobody has tested it on an HVAC owner.
Practitioner Sales industry data
Big datasets from companies like Gong and Cognism. Useful for targets, but they don't publish their methods or define "success" the same way.
Hypothesis Our own idea
Makes sense, has no study behind it. We test it in our own tracker before we trust it.

Psychology · the HVAC owner

Five things are stacked against you

Our default buyer: the owner of an established, family-run shop in Fairfield or New Haven County, roughly 10 to 45 employees, often the one who answers or returns the call.

  1. They didn't ask for the call. You're interrupting a working day, often in the truck or on a job.
  2. They decide in seconds whether you're credible and relevant. Most sales calls fail before the second sentence.
  3. Free cuts both ways. It sparks interest and triggers "what's the catch?"
  4. Saying yes can cost them. Logins, staff time, data, or someone else's approval.
  5. The person who answers may not decide. Often it's the office manager or dispatcher.

Practitioner Gartner's survey of 632 B2B buyers: 73% actively avoid suppliers who send irrelevant outreach. (Skews to larger companies.)

Tap a worry. Hear the thought.

"If I say yes, I'll get chased forever."
It's yours to keep, and a no ends follow-up.
"I don't have time for a meeting."
Name the exact time: 15 minutes, or a text with the file.
"Who's going to do the fixes?"
A fix list they can hand to their current person.
"What are you doing with my data?"
The first step uses only public information. No logins.
"I already have a web person."
It's a second opinion, not a replacement.
"Free means junk."
Show the method and the date. Show the work.
Before your first dial, say each answer out loud once, in your own words.
Psychology · you, the caller

Calm comes from knowing the odds

, here's what the research means for how you carry yourself on the phone.

1. A "no" is the base rate
Even top teams book on about 11% of dials (Cognism). At the 2026 average of 2.7%, almost every dial ends without a meeting. You're not failing; you're sampling. Practitioner
2. Silence usually isn't about you
Low connect rates usually mean bad phone data, not bad reps. If nobody's picking up, fix the list before you fix yourself.
3. Your voice is the first demo
People form fast impressions of warmth and competence from very short slices of behavior (Ambady et al., thin-slice judgments of sales managers). On the phone, your voice is the only signal. Slow down, smile while speaking, pause after the reason for calling. A calm delivery makes the free offer sound generous instead of desperate. Direct, partly
Forced cheerfulness sounds fake. We couldn't read the full paper, so don't quote its numbers.
4. You're bringing a finished thing
You aren't asking for a favor. You're offering a specific, bounded piece of work the owner keeps either way. That changes your posture from asking to giving.
5. One clarify, then let go
Each objection gets one clarifying answer. If the second answer is still no, accept it and end politely. That protects the owner's trust and your energy.
6. Daily block, no exceptions
Jeb Blount's rule: prospect every day. Block the time, track dials → conversations → meetings, and log every call within the hour.
Before every dial

What the owner hears

A neighbor with a reason, or a script. They can tell within seconds.

What you feel

An urge to rush or soften. The fix is the same both times: slow down, give the reason, pause.

Call structure

Five steps. About two minutes.

If it goes well, this is the whole call. Tap a step to train it.

The longest step is the one where they talk.

Following our Promise Chain method, every script leads with the owner's problem, not with AI.

Step 1 · 10 seconds

Name the reason first

Practitioner Test first

In the first ten seconds: your name, Starwell, that this is a cold call, and the one specific reason you're calling them.

Hi Craig, this is with Starwell in . This is a cold call, so I'll be quick. I'm calling about one thing I noticed on your website.
Removes "who is this and what do they want?" so they can judge relevance.

The numbers

  • Gong: a permission-based opener at 11.18% success vs 2.15% for "Did I catch you at a bad time?" (Gong doesn't define success or publish its method.)
  • Stating the reason for the call: 2.1× meetings (Gong).
  • 73% of B2B buyers avoid irrelevant outreach (Gartner, 632 buyers).

Quick check: best opener?

When it backfires

The stated reason is generic ("helping businesses grow"). Or "this is a cold call" comes out like a memorized gimmick. Say it like a neighbor.

Owner hears

"Unknown number. Sales? Scam? What do they want?" Uncertainty is the enemy. Remove it.

You feel

The pull to soften with "bad time?" Don't. Honesty about the cold call is the softener.

Record yourself saying the opener three times. Keep the version that sounds least like a script.
Step 2 · 15 seconds

Lead with one true thing

Transferred Test first

One specific, checkable fact about their shop, found before you dial. It proves relevance and homework in a single sentence.

Your site says the office closes at [VERIFY: time], and I didn't see a way to book after that. I'm curious how evening calls get handled.

Evidence: field experiments found adding a name to email subject lines raised opens and later outcomes (Sahni et al., 2018). Gartner's buyers punish irrelevance. No peer-reviewed cold call study exists.

Step 3 · 30–60 seconds

Ask about last week, then adapt

Direct · High Strongest evidence we have

Ask one question about something that actually happened recently. Listen. Shape the rest of the call around the answer instead of running the full pitch.

Quick question before I say anything else: when a call came in after hours last week, what happened to it?

Franke & Park (2006): meta-analysis of 155 samples, 31,000+ salespeople. Adaptive selling improved self-rated, manager-rated and objective performance. Customer orientation alone improved only self-rated performance, and it has an optimum (Homburg et al., 2011). Field sales, not cold calls.

Three moves after they answer

  1. One follow-up. "You said Sarah handles the callbacks. What happens on her day off?" Follow-up questions increase liking (Huang et al., 2017). Transferred
  2. Ask for their expertise. "You've run this shop for 30 years. What's the one thing new homeowners get wrong when they call you?" Advice seekers are seen as more competent (Brooks et al., 2015). Transferred
  3. Let them do the math. "You said about five calls go to voicemail on a busy night. What's a typical repair worth to you?" Never invent the figure. Transferred · weak

Last week or someday?

Future questions get polite guesses. Recent-event questions get facts.

"What happened to the calls that came in Saturday?"
"Would you ever consider an AI receptionist?"
"Who posted the last job photo on your page?"
"If we could double your leads, would you be interested?"
Three or more questions before you give anything back turns into an interrogation.

Owner hears

Someone responding to their shop. The free offer is chosen after their answer, so it fits the leak they described.

You feel

Relief. You don't have to guess which service to pitch. They'll tell you.

Step 4 · 15 seconds

Offer a thing, not a meeting

Four parts, said in about 15 seconds.

  1. A bounded deliverable. A named thing with a clear size. Never "a free consultation." Transferred
    Concrete offers are processed more fluently, and fluent information is judged as truer and more likable (Alter & Oppenheimer, 2009).
  2. Why it's free, in one sentence. Same words every time, then stop talking. Transferred
    When people spot a persuasion attempt, they hunt for the hidden motive (Friestad & Wright, 1994). Name it yourself.
  3. One limit. What it won't do. Transferred
    Admitting a drawback can raise credibility (Eisend, 2006, two-sided advertising meta-analysis).
  4. No access, no work. Built from public info only. Hypothesis
The why-free sentence: "It's free because we're building our HVAC client base, and showing real work is the fastest way to earn a second conversation."
I made a one-page snapshot. It takes 15 minutes to walk through, and you keep it either way. I can't promise you'll show up in ChatGPT; nobody controls that. What I can show you is what it says today.

Give one finding on the call, free, right away. Never hold the snapshot hostage for a meeting.

Owner hears

"I can picture it, I know how long it takes, and I don't owe anything." Three worries answered at once.

You feel

Steady. One sentence of why, then silence. Long explanations sound like hiding something.

Step 5 · 15 seconds

Pin a date, or take a clean no

Tuesday at 7:30 before the trucks go out? I'll text you the snapshot link the night before, and it's 15 minutes. If it's not useful, just say so and I won't follow up.

Why each piece is there

Transferred · High Date + agenda
Implementation intentions ("if X, then I do Y") make follow-through far more likely: meta-analysis of 94 tests, d = .65 (Gollwitzer & Sheeran, 2006). Health and study goals, not sales meetings. Agree on day, time, place or link, who attends, what they'll see, then send it in writing.
Transferred · Mixed Freedom to say no
Reactance: people push back when their freedom feels threatened. A 2023 meta-analysis of 52 experiments found g = 0.44, but the seven low-bias studies showed a near-zero effect (g = 0.11). Say it once, mean it, and never follow up after a no.
Transferred · Low Start small
Foot-in-the-door is real but small and conditional (Burger, 1999). Ask for 15 minutes, not an hour. No hidden escalation ("while I have you…").

Build the confirmation text

Early morning or evening, outside busy hours, is our guess for owner-led shops. It's untested; the tracker will tell us.

Owner hears

A small, finished event with an end time, not an open-ended meeting that eats the morning.

You feel

Tempted to push after a soft no. Don't. Pushing for a date after a soft no reads as pressure.

Psychology · free

Free helps. Free also hurts.

  • It pulls. Making a cheap chocolate free instead of one cent shifted choices sharply (Shampanier, Mazar & Ariely, 2007). But that was cheap, low-stakes items. Who touches your website or phones is not low-stakes.
  • It can cheapen. Offering an item free can make people value it less (Raghubir, 2004).
  • It raises suspicion. Sensing a tactic, people look for the hidden motive (Friestad & Wright, 1994).
Free works when it's specific, small, clearly ours, and clearly explained. It hurts when it's vague, unexplained, or looks like a hook.

Protect the paid work

Showing the work behind a result raised perceived value in online experiments (Buell & Norton, 2011, "labor illusion"). Transferred

The three-week web (Jason Bay)

  1. Same day: call with the reason first, leave a voicemail, then send the free asset by email (and LinkedIn video where it fits).
  2. Weeks 1–3: follow up across call, email and LinkedIn. The goal of every touch is a meeting, not a close.

Owner's lens

Bookings alone aren't success

What we measure, in order:

  1. Qualified conversations: reached the decision maker and they answered the discovery question.
  2. Accepted next steps: a dated walkthrough, or an agreed text plus a follow-up date.
  3. Meetings actually held.
  4. Qualified opportunities: a named problem, a cost the owner states, and agreement to discuss a paid pilot.
  5. Paid conversions.

Also every week: negative reactions (hangups, "remove me", complaints) and hours spent on free work.

Plug in your week

Benchmarks: Gong (connect, next steps per conversation), our planning rule (40–45 dials per meeting), our 3-hour free cap proposal.

Non-negotiable

Lines we never cross

Never cold call with our own AI voice agent. In February 2024 the FCC ruled AI-generated voices count as artificial voices under the TCPA, so robocall consent rules apply. A person makes every cold call.

Not yet researched: Connecticut's own telemarketing rules and rules for calling mobile numbers. Check before scaling.

Proof, for now

Mention other customers only when the result is real, similar and permitted. Until we have HVAC results, the free deliverable is the proof. (Social-proof research is fragile: the famous hotel towel study failed to replicate.)
