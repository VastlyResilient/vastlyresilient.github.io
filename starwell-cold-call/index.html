Every ring gets answered

A 24/7 virtual receptionist for the rings the shop misses: busy, on a rooftop, after hours, weekends. It greets the caller, asks the right HVAC questions, books the request or transfers a real emergency to the on-call tech, then texts the owner a summary.

Words to use
With owners, say "phone answering system" or "virtual receptionist." Never open with "AI." Save tool names for the setup conversation.

Why it matters (sourced)

  • 27% of calls to home-services businesses went unanswered (Invoca, 2024). Another platform measured 14% (CallRail, 2025).
  • Under 3% of callers sent to voicemail leave a message (Invoca platform data).
  • 82% of consumers say they'll call a competitor if you don't answer (CallRail survey, 2025).
  • 46% of home-services phone leads convert during the call (Invoca, 60M+ calls, 2025).

The call flow

  1. Greeting + recording notice (Connecticut requires all-party notice, CGS §52-570d).
  2. Emergency first: gas smell, CO alarm, no heat in extreme weather. If unsafe, leave the home and call 911 or the gas utility, then live transfer to the on-call tech.
  3. Booking details, one question at a time: name, number, address, system, issue, best time.
  4. Maintenance plans and estimates: capture interest, never invent prices.
  5. FAQs only from what the shop published or approved.
  6. Close + summary texted and emailed to the owner.

Week one

Day 1–2 learn the shop · Day 2–3 build · Day 3–4 practice calls with the owner · Day 5 soft launch (after-hours or overflow) · Day 6–7 tune from real transcripts. It starts on a test number; his real line forwards only when he says yes.

Do the math with HIS numbers
Missed calls × share that were real jobs × his booking rate × his average ticket. Never quote a dollar figure of our own; no source with a stated method gives a reliable cost per missed HVAC call.

If you can teach it, you know it

The goal isn't memorizing. It's understanding each system well enough to explain it to an owner, or a new teammate, in plain words.

The explain-it formula

One sentence (what it does for him) + one analogy (from his world) + one proof (a sourced fact, labeled).

Methods that work

  • Transferred Test yourself instead of rereading. Practicing recall beats restudying for long-term memory (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006). That's why there's a self-check card next.
  • Transferred Learn as if you'll teach it. People who expected to teach material learned it better (Nestojko et al., 2014).
  • Transferred Teach-back. Doctors ask patients to explain instructions back in their own words to confirm understanding (AHRQ health literacy toolkit). Do the same: explain a system to a teammate, and let them ask "why?"
  • Practitioner The Feynman technique: explain it simply, notice where you get stuck, go back and fill the gap.
  • Analogies from the trade: ductwork, furnace, thermostat, vents. Owners understand systems; speak in theirs.

Psychology · you, the caller

Your confidence kit

, here's what the research says about the person on your end of the phone. Most of it is good news.

1. They'll like you more than you think
Across five studies, people after a first conversation consistently underestimated how much the other person liked them. It's called the liking gap (Boothby et al., 2018). The call felt shakier to you than it sounded to them. Transferred
2. Call the butterflies "excited"
People who said "I'm excited" before singing, speaking or doing math felt more excited, and in the original studies performed better than people who said "I'm calm" (Brooks, 2014). Honest note: a 2025 replication confirmed the feeling, but observers didn't rate speeches better. It reliably helps how you feel; the performance boost is less certain. Transferred
3. Your voice is the first demo
People judge warmth and competence from very short slices of behavior (Ambady et al., thin-slice studies of sales managers). On the phone your voice is the whole picture. Slow down, smile while you talk, pause after you say why you're calling.
4. You're holding a gift
You aren't asking for a favor. You're offering a finished package the owner keeps either way. That's a giving posture, and it sounds like one.
5. "Not now" is about timing
Most owners are mid-job when you call. A no usually means "not this minute." Thank them, end kindly, and move on with your energy intact.
6. Rhythm beats mood
Jeb Blount: prospect every day. Block the time, dial through it, log each call within the hour. Momentum does the rest.

Before every line you say, ask: how does this sound to a busy owner standing on a roof? That one habit fixes most cold-call mistakes.

Transferred In negotiation experiments, people who actively took the other side's perspective found more deals that worked for both sides (Galinsky et al., 2008). Carnegie said it simply: see things from the other person's point of view.

A real owner's warning

I love when sales teams reach out acting like they know everything about my HVAC company and my personal background, then proceed to try and sell me staffing services or marketing analytics.
An HVAC owner in Tampa, on X, March 2026 (from our pricing research)

Homework should show respect, not surveillance. One true fact about the shop, said humbly, with where you found it. Then listen, and offer something that fits what he said.

Observation habits

  • Listen for his words and use them back: if he says "the phones are nuts," don't say "call volume."
  • Notice the background: a truck, a job site, a crying baby. Offer to call back at a better time.
  • Notice energy: short answers mean busy, not hostile. Get to the point.
  • Notice pride: years in business, his crew, his reviews. Honor it sincerely.

His view

"Is this person going to cost me time or save me time?"

Your job

Make it obvious within 30 seconds that you're there to save him time.

Psychology · connection

People like people who like them

When you've done your homework, the owner hears it in your first sentence: I see your shop, I respect your time, I'm here for you.

What the research says

  • Transferred Reciprocity of liking: people tend to like those they believe like them (Montoya & Insko, 2008).
  • Transferred Shared ground: a meta-analysis of 313 studies found perceived similarity drives liking (Montoya, Horton & Kirchner, 2008). Local, trades, family business: use it when it's real.
  • Direct In B2B: buyers' trust in a salesperson was tied to expertise, likability and similarity (Doney & Cannon, 1997). Honest note: that trust didn't decide the immediate purchase once past performance was counted. Liking opens the door; the quality of the package walks through it.
  • Transferred Say where you found it: personal details landed well when people knew how they were gathered, and felt invasive when hidden (Aguirre et al., 2015). So: "I was on your website this morning…"
Know the shop, not the person's private life. Website, Google profile and reviews, business social pages, careers page, local news. Never home, family, or personal accounts.

Great homework or too far?

Step 1 · 10 seconds

Say why you're calling

Your name, StarWell, that it's a cold call, and where you found the one thing you noticed.

Hi Craig, this is with StarWell, here in Connecticut. This is a cold call, so I'll be quick. I was on your website this morning and noticed one thing I think could help.
Honest, local, and it says where you looked. He can relax and listen.

Practitioner Gong reports a permission-style opener at about 11% success versus about 2% for "Did I catch you at a bad time?" (method unpublished). Stating your reason goes with about 2.1× more meetings.

Step 3 · 30–60 seconds

Ask about last week, then listen

Direct · strongest evidence we have

One question about something that actually happened. Then let his answer pick which gift you lead with.

When that first cold night hit last week, how did the phones go after hours?

A meta-analysis of 155 samples and 31,000+ salespeople found adaptive selling, shaping the conversation around the buyer, improved performance, including objective sales (Franke & Park, 2006).

Three moves after he answers

  1. One follow-up. "You said Sarah handles callbacks. What happens on her day off?" Follow-up questions increase liking (Huang et al., 2017).
  2. Ask for his expertise. "Thirty years fixing furnaces: what do homeowners get wrong when they call?" People who ask for advice are seen as more competent (Brooks et al., 2015).
  3. Let him do the math. "What's a typical no-heat repair worth to you?" Never supply the number yourself.

Last week or someday?

"What happened to the calls that came in Saturday night?"
"Would you ever consider an AI receptionist?"
"How many no-heat calls came in after 6 this week?"

Owner hears

Someone curious about his shop. He gets to talk about his work, and most owners enjoy that.

You feel

Relief. You don't have to guess what to offer. He'll tell you.

Step 4 · 20 seconds

Lead with what fits

Offer the one gift that matches what he just told you, in owner words. Mention the rest in a single line. The email carries the details.

We built a demo line that answers like your office would, books the call, and texts you who called. It's a separate test number, so nothing touches your phones. Call it tonight and hear it yourself.
I'll send you one page showing how your shop comes up when people search for furnace repair in [town], or ask their phone who to call. Plain English, with what we already fixed for you.
We built a fresh version of your website, made to turn the people who find you into phone calls. Take a look; it's yours either way.
We'll run your Facebook, Instagram and TikTok for you: a few posts and short videos a week, built from your real jobs. You approve the plan in about fifteen minutes a month.
I'll put the rest of the package in the email too. It's all free, because we're new to Connecticut HVAC and we'd rather show you than tell you. If you love it, maybe we work together down the road. If not, it's still yours.
One gift in focus, the rest in one line, why-free, then stop talking.

Why this works

  • Transferred A named, concrete gift is easier to picture, and easy-to-process ideas are judged as more true (Alter & Oppenheimer, 2009).
  • Transferred Naming the motive yourself answers "what's the catch" before he asks (Friestad & Wright, 1994).
  • Transferred One honest limit makes the rest more believable (Eisend, 2006): "Nobody can promise where Google ranks you, but I can show you exactly where you stand."

Owner hears

"Something built for me, I can see it in two minutes, and I don't owe anything."

You feel

The urge to explain all four in detail. Resist it. Less said, more wanted.

Step 5 · 15 seconds

Grab the email, pin the date

What's the best email for you? I'll send a short note within the hour with all three things. And so I can show you around it, how's Tuesday at 7:30, before the trucks go out? Fifteen minutes. If it's not useful, just say so and I won't follow up.
  • Transferred · High Specific when-and-where plans strongly raise follow-through (meta-analysis of 94 tests, d = .65; Gollwitzer & Sheeran, 2006). Day, time, length, in writing.
  • Transferred · Mixed "You're free to say no" may lower pushback, though the best-controlled studies show a small effect. Say it once and mean it.
  • Transferred Start small: 15 minutes, not an hour (Burger, 1999).

Build the recap email

Send it within the hour, while the call is fresh. Short, owner words, no jargon. Fill in the real links before sending.

Psychology · conversation

Tools that make people feel heard

These are the most useful conversation habits for a cold call, with why each works. Use them to serve, never to manipulate.

1. Ask, then follow up
People who ask more questions, especially follow-ups, are liked more (Huang et al., 2017). You said Saturdays are rough. What happens to those calls? Transferred
2. Repeat his words back
In a restaurant study, servers who repeated orders back word for word got bigger tips (van Baaren et al., 2003). Mirroring his exact words shows you heard him. "A couple went to voicemail Saturday." Got it. Transferred
3. Summarize before you offer
Sum up what he said in one sentence, then offer the gift that fits. It proves the offer is about him, not a script. So the days are covered, but nights and Saturdays slip. That's exactly what the phone demo is for.
4. Ask for his advice
People who ask for advice are seen as more competent (Brooks, Gino & Schweitzer, 2015), and owners love talking about their trade. Transferred
5. Use analogies from his world
"It's like a dispatcher who never sleeps." Analogies turn new ideas into familiar ones. Pick from the trade: ductwork, thermostats, service calls.
6. Make him the hero
In every story, the homeowner and the owner are the heroes; StarWell is the guide. Never the other way around. Practitioner
7. Pause on purpose
After the reason for calling and after the why-free sentence, stop. Silence gives him room to think, and shows you're not rushing him.
8. Use his name, sparingly
Carnegie: a person's name is to him the sweetest sound. Once or twice is warm; every sentence is a sales tactic.
9. Admit what you don't know
"I'm not sure. I'll find out and text you by tonight." Honest uncertainty, said with confidence, keeps trust (Gaertig & Simmons, 2018). Then keep the promise. Transferred
The test for every tool: would I be comfortable if he knew exactly why I said it? If yes, it's service. If no, drop it.

No catch, for real

"No strings" has to be literally true. Here's how we keep it that way, and why it matters.

Free that feels valuable

  • Transferred Free can make people value things less (Raghubir, 2004). Show the work: people valued results more when they saw the effort behind them (Buell & Norton, 2011). In the walkthrough, show what you looked at and what you built.
  • Every owner leaves with something useful, even a "no": the recap email always includes the one true thing you found.

Serve both. This just tells you who's likely to want ongoing help.

Our way with every question

Answer like a good steward

Every question an owner asks is a gift: it means he's thinking about it. We answer the same way every time, with honesty, warmth and care for his shop.

The four-step answer

  1. Thank. "Great question." He should feel smart for asking.
  2. Truth. The plain answer, including what we don't know yet.
  3. Tie. Connect it back to his shop and what he told you.
  4. Turn. Hand it back with a gentle question or the next small step.
Begin in a friendly way. Warmth first, always.
Let him do most of the talking. Be a good listener.
Avoid arguments. You can't win one, even when you're right.
Respect his opinion. Never say "you're wrong."
If we're wrong, admit it quickly.
Talk in terms of his interests, and see it from his side.

When he asks "what's it cost?"

Price questions are good news

If he's asking about price on day one, he's picturing using it. Answer it straight. Never dodge it.

What to say

Great question, and I'll be straight with you. The package we built is free, and it stays yours. If you ever want us to keep it running or take more off your plate, most shops start around $300 to $400 a month, in writing, with no surprises. Your exact quote depends on your setup, and we'd rather see it work for you first. And if it doesn't make sense for you, you keep everything.
Thank, truth, tie, turn. Free stays free, the "it depends" is explained, and he's in control.

If he wants the details

Care keeps your site, Google profile and monthly report handled, around $300 to $400. Some shops add ongoing search work or the phone answering on their real line, and those are higher. Everything's on one printed card, month to month, and you'd have your exact number in writing within a day.

Full tiers and why they're fair: Services → Pricing, for the team.

What the price depends on (say it plainly)

  • How many locations and trucks he runs.
  • How many calls come in, and when.
  • How much he wants us to run for him.

Never

  • Dodge it, or say "we'll get to that."
  • Make up a number to sound sure.
  • Discount on the spot, or say "only if you sign today."
  • Let "free" quietly turn into a bill.
  • Change a quoted price without talking to him first. We don't go back on our word.

Honest answer or slippery?

"The package is free and stays yours. Ongoing help is quoted per shop, in writing, before anything."
"Let's not worry about price right now."
"Oh, it's like fifty bucks a month, tops."
"I'd rather give you a real number than guess. You'll have it in writing within a day."
Why it works
Yea, yea; Nay, nay (Matthew 5:37). People trust an honest "it depends" said with confidence (Gaertig & Simmons, 2018), and openness about cost builds trust (Mohan, Buell & John, 2020).
